> "When both chokepoints are contested at once, the corridor you can still count on stops being a convenience and becomes the business."

Three weeks ago in Issue 005 I wrote that a quieter week was not the same as a settled one. I did not expect to be proved right this quickly or this comprehensively. What was a tanker war between two states has become, in the space of a week, a broader assault on merchant shipping. Iran says it attacked ten vessels. UKMTO logged several hit by disabling fire in a single night. A tanker carrying two million barrels of Iraqi fuel oil is on fire. These are not military targets in any meaningful sense. Freight conditions this week are extraordinary, but they should not be mistaken for a healthy market. Dirty and clean tanker earnings have strengthened sharply, particularly on Middle East Gulf-linked trades. The market is pricing fewer willing ships, war-risk exposure, longer voyages and more difficult insurance discussions. High rates earned in these conditions are compensation for danger, not evidence of prosperity.

That is precisely why our own corridor matters more this quarter than it ever has. Tonnage, cargo and bunkering demand are being pushed toward Malacca and Singapore not because we are cheap but because we are predictable. Predictability is a standard we have to keep earning on every job — through screening that holds up, documentation that survives scrutiny, and STS execution that no counterparty or underwriter can question. My instruction is unchanged and now more urgent: no Hormuz-linked nomination without owner-level authorisation and current-week war-risk confirmation, and no STS counterparty accepted without a full blacklist and transit-history check.

## This Week in Review

The week's principal developments, 5–11 September 2026.

**9–10 September · Reuters / UKMTO — Iran claims attacks on ten vessels near Hormuz.** Iran stated it attacked ten vessels near the Strait of Hormuz, described by Reuters as the largest declared wave of tit-for-tat shipping attacks by either side since the war began. UKMTO reported several merchant vessels in the northern Gulf and Gulf of Oman hit by disabling fire overnight, and could not confirm casualties or environmental impact. A vessel was sighted listing at anchor 24nm from Port Rashid, UAE, possibly taking on water after a projectile strike, and a cargo ship was struck approximately 28nm southeast of al-Faw, Iraq. The Panama-flagged, Greek-owned tanker NEW ANDROS, carrying about two million barrels of Iraqi fuel oil under charter to the Iraqi Oil Tankers Company, caught fire.

**8 September · CENTCOM — US strikes five Iranian crude carriers.** US Central Command struck five Iranian crude carriers late on 8 September: the Kaviz, Charminar, Horizon 1 and Riesco in the Gulf of Oman, and the Derya near Kharg Island. Iran responded with an attack on Jordan's al-Azraq airbase. The strikes followed three Iranian tankers hit on 5 September, which in turn followed Iranian ballistic missile launches toward a US aircraft carrier and a destroyer operating near the Strait.

**September · Al Jazeera / MarineTraffic — an environmental tinderbox: 42 tankers waiting, 18 laden.** MarineTraffic data showed 42 tankers waiting to transit the Strait as of Monday, 18 of them carrying oil, with a combined estimated capacity of roughly 1.76 million cubic metres — about 11.1 million barrels, or close to 11% of daily world oil demand. Analysts warn that a major tanker strike or sinking could release millions of litres into the Gulf, contaminating coastlines, fisheries and fragile marine ecosystems. The half-sunken tanker Caroline Bezengi is already cited as a live example of the risk.

**September · gCaptain / BBC / Hellenic Shipping News — Houthis reported to have seized the port of Mocha.** Houthi forces are reported to have seized the strategic Red Sea port of Mocha, tightening their proximity to the Bab el-Mandeb chokepoint and raising the operational risk profile for vessels transiting between the Indian Ocean and the Red Sea. Whether the advance materially closes Bab el-Mandeb to commercial traffic remains an open question, but it removes much of the comfort that the Red Sea routing offered as an alternative to Hormuz.

**September · Ship & Bunker / CENTCOM — US says Hormuz mines cleared; independent counts still thin.** Admiral Brad Cooper said US forces have cleared Iranian sea mines from Strait of Hormuz shipping lanes and assisted more than 1,500 ships transiting in recent months. Independent transit counts remain far below pre-crisis norms: PortWatch's most recent published day (6 September) recorded six transits against a pre-crisis baseline of about 85 per day. As in Issue 006, we note the gap between official and independently tracked figures without resolving it.

Crude has repriced decisively. The move accelerated on 9 September, when Brent broke US$100 for the first time in our reporting series, and again on 10 September with a US$6.42 single-session gain. The immediate drivers are the widening attacks on merchant shipping and the loss of confidence in any near-term restoration of normal Hormuz flows.

This week crude, clean freight, dirty freight and bunkers have all moved sharply in the same direction at the same time — a pattern more consistent with a systemic risk repricing than with a supply headline.

## Geopolitical & Route Risk

Both regional chokepoints contested simultaneously.

**From tanker war to general assault.** The qualitative change this week is that the conflict has stopped discriminating. Through Issues 004 to 006 the pattern was state-on-state: US strikes on Iranian tankers, Iranian strikes on US-linked vessels and warships. This week Iran declared attacks on ten vessels near the Strait, and UKMTO reported several merchant ships hit by disabling fire overnight across the northern Gulf and Gulf of Oman — including a vessel listing at anchor 24nm from Port Rashid and a cargo ship struck 28nm southeast of al-Faw. The NEW ANDROS, a Panama-flagged, Greek-owned tanker on charter to the Iraqi Oil Tankers Company, is alight with roughly two million barrels aboard. None of those profiles fits a military targeting rationale.

**The alternative corridor just narrowed.** Through previous issues, the Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb routing has functioned as the imperfect but available alternative to Hormuz — the reason container majors were able to contemplate a Suez return in Issue 005. The reported Houthi seizure of Mocha changes that calculus. With both chokepoints contested at once, the practical consequence for our readership is that Malacca-routed and intra-Asia trade carries a scarcity value it has not had before.

## Sanctions & Compliance Watch

**Developments — week ending 11 September 2026**

Blacklist and STS contagion, standing from Issue 006: Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority blacklist of 45 tankers — including vessels linked to ADNOC Logistics & Services, Navig8 Tankers and Saudi Arabia's Bahri — remains in force, with the stated threat that any vessel conducting an STS transfer with a listed ship may itself be added. Nothing this week has softened that position, and the widening attacks make enforcement risk more credible rather than less.

Dark activity and identity risk: Windward reported an OFAC-sanctioned 281-metre container vessel that went dark on 25 March 2026 and remained off AIS for approximately 166 days, reappearing on 7 September and subsequently heading east toward the Gulf of Oman with Port Klang declared as its destination. Extended dark periods of this length, followed by a declared Malaysian destination, are precisely the profile our screening is designed to catch.

Direct relevance to our operations: a vessel emerging from a 166-day AIS gap and declaring a Port Klang destination is a live example of the risk sitting on our own doorstep. Any STS or bunkering counterparty presenting with a material recent dark period should be escalated to owner level before acceptance, regardless of how the paperwork reads.
