## CEO Market Commentary

> **Capt. Dinesh Naidu KC — Founder & Chairman, DDS Marine Group**
>
> "A quieter week is not the same as a settled one. We should read it that way."

After the week we recorded in Issue 004, I am glad to have less to report on the human cost side of this crisis. We have not identified a fresh casualty or seizure in the seven days to 28 August. That is genuinely good news, and I do not want to bury it under caveats. But a quieter week is not the same as a settled one, and owners should not read the calm as a green light.

What has actually changed is diplomatic positioning, not operational reality. Iran's state media is reporting that Tehran and Oman have reached the outline of an understanding on managed transit, and the IRGC's own spokesman has said the Strait could reopen if the United States "stops obstructing the process." That is a negotiating posture, not a signed agreement, and Kpler's own count still shows only five vessels transiting Hormuz on 25 August, every one of them moving under Iran's self-declared Unilateral Scheme rather than any internationally recognised transit right. OFAC's own alert, updated on 24 August, continues to warn our industry against paying Iranian "toll" or permit demands for safe passage. Nothing about the underlying legal conflict for owners has been resolved; the volume of public noise around it has simply increased.

The development I would ask every reader to sit with is closer to home. On 25 August, Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore jointly reaffirmed that the Straits of Malacca and Singapore will stay open under the transit-passage provisions of UNCLOS. Singapore's Transport Minister called it a reminder that "open sea lanes can never be taken for granted." That statement exists because our region is now absorbing meaningful redirected tonnage from operators unwilling to test Hormuz, and because the eyes of the shipping world are increasingly on how well we handle that responsibility. TD2, Middle East Gulf to Singapore, remains one of the most valuable crude routes on the Baltic board this week at US$670,287/day. That value is not an accident of geography; it is a vote of confidence in this corridor that we have to keep earning.

My instruction to our teams is unchanged from Issue 004: treat every Hormuz-linked nomination as an owner-level decision, not a master's judgement call, and keep our own STS and screening standards visibly ahead of the corridor everyone else is avoiding. A calmer week is the right moment to tighten discipline, not relax it.

## Geopolitical & Route Risk

Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb and Malacca — three chokepoints, three different trajectories this week.

| Area | Current Signal | Commercial Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Strait of Hormuz | Traffic still thin (5 vessels, 25 Aug, per Kpler) but Iran-Oman reportedly close to a managed-transit framework. | No change yet to the underlying legal conflict for owners; OFAC's 24 Aug alert still warns against paying Iranian transit tolls. |
| Bab el-Mandeb | Sporadic Houthi threat persists; no new confirmed incident identified this window. | Continued elevated war-risk premium on Red Sea routings. |
| Malacca & Singapore Straits | Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore jointly reaffirmed open, safe transit under UNCLOS on 25 Aug. | Reinforces the region's position as the credible alternative corridor; raises the bar on our own screening and STS standards. |

**Where the Iran-Oman talks actually stand.** Iran's semi-official Fars News Agency reports that Tehran and Oman have spent the past month in negotiations and reached conclusions accepted by both sides, with an IRGC spokesperson saying the Strait could reopen if the United States "stops obstructing the process" and accepts Iran's conditions. That is a long way from a signed, verifiable agreement, and the same reporting notes an Indian tanker, the *Haana*, turned back from the southern Oman Corridor after an IRGC warning on the same day the talks were described as advanced — a reminder that the Unilateral Scheme, not any negotiated right, still governs practical passage.

**Container shipping's cautious return to Suez.** Separately, container majors are testing a return to the Red Sea/Suez route after roughly three years of diversion around Africa. MSC has officially announced a partial return; Maersk/Hapag-Lloyd's Gemini network and the CMA CGM/COSCO/Evergreen/OOCL Ocean Alliance are each running partial Suez services. Some commentators suggest normalisation is plausible by end-2026, though some services will likely keep routing via Africa to absorb released capacity.

> No new crew casualty or vessel seizure identified this window; however Iran's Unilateral Scheme still governs Hormuz passage and no binding framework exists. DDS recommends maintaining current owner-level authorisation and war-risk protocols pending written confirmation of any Iran-Oman terms.

## Sanctions & Compliance Watch

**Developments — week ending 28 August 2026**

- **OFAC alert update, 24 August:** Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control issued an update to its earlier alert on "Sanctions Risks of Iranian Demands for Strait of Hormuz Passage," refreshing guidance first issued 1 May 2026 in light of continued Iranian demands for transit permits and "toll" payments. OFAC continues to strongly encourage maritime service providers to conduct enhanced due diligence on any vessel attempting a Hormuz transit. The same day, OFAC issued a "Notice of Suspension of Certain Iranian Transactions and Sanctions Regulations General Licenses" — owners should confirm with counsel whether any general licence they rely on is affected.
- **Standing designation activity:** on 29 July 2026, OFAC designated PGMIC and Hormuz Safe under Executive Order 13902 for operating in the financial sector of the Iranian economy — a reminder that Iran-linked designations continue on a rolling basis independent of the diplomatic headlines.

This creates direct legal conflict for owners that has not changed since Issue 004: paying a fee demanded by Iran's asserted permit-and-toll regime may create sanctions exposure; refusing may invite the kind of seizure risk recorded in our last issue. Obtain written owner and insurer positions before nominating tonnage to the Strait, and do not treat improved diplomatic tone as a substitute for a confirmed framework.

## Southeast Asia & STS Operations

This is the most consequential week for our home region since we began this publication. On 25 August, Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore — the Littoral States of the Straits of Malacca and Singapore — issued a joint statement reaffirming their "shared commitment to keep the Straits open and safe for international shipping," explicitly affirming the Straits' status under UNCLOS transit-passage provisions. Singapore's Transport Minister, Jeffrey Siow, told an Indonesia-Malaysia-Singapore forum that "events in the Middle East this year reminded the world that open sea lanes can never be taken for granted," adding that the three states "will endeavour to keep the Straits of Malacca and Singapore free, open and safe for international shipping."

| Market | Why It Matters This Week |
|---|---|
| Malaysia | Home to Port Klang, Malacca, Muar, Linggi and the Johor STS/bunkering corridor. The 25 Aug joint statement puts our home waters directly in the frame of the global Hormuz conversation. |
| Singapore | TD2 (ME Gulf/Singapore) remains the strongest dirty tanker route on the board at Ws630.56, worth US$670,287/day. |
| Indonesia | Co-signatory to the 25 Aug pledge; Batam and Karimun remain active in STS and bunkering. |
| Thailand | Gulf of Thailand distribution continues to benefit from trade diversion as a secondary regional routing option. |
| Vietnam | Growing product-import demand; a bellwether for intra-Asia clean tanker and MR patterns as the Pacific MR basket firms. |

> **DDS VIEW**
>
> A formal, named pledge from three governments to keep our home corridor open is not something we should treat as routine. It is a public statement that the world is watching how Southeast Asia handles the volume redirected away from Hormuz. The commercial case for rigorous, documented, verifiable STS execution has never been stronger, and this week gives us a public benchmark — set by our own governments — to be measured against.
