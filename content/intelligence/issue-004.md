## This Week at a Glance

The 60-day Islamabad MoU lapsed at midnight on 17 August with no deal, no extension and no talks. Within 48 hours Iran had begun enforcing a permit-and-toll regime through both kinetic action and vessel seizure: a chief engineer was killed aboard the bulk carrier *MINOAN DIGNITY* in the southern corridor, and the tanker *AMARA* was boarded and seized near Qeshm Island — the first confirmed Iranian seizure since 22 June. Brent has crossed US$93 and Murban has broken US$100. Most telling for our readers: China's two largest state tanker operators are now keeping more than 100 VLCCs out of both Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb on Beijing's guidance, and three China-linked tankers aborted Hormuz transits inside the reporting window. This is no longer a risk-pricing market. It is a market where major fleets are declining to transit at any price.

- **Diplomacy:** The 60-day MoU window lapsed 17 August. Tehran declared it nullified and asserted mandatory transit permits and maritime environmental tolls; Washington rejected the claim and maintained convoy escorts and blockade enforcement.
- **Crew fatality:** *MINOAN DIGNITY* (IMO 9294484), Liberia-flagged bulk carrier, struck by an unknown projectile in its engine room 0.5nm off the Omani coast overnight 17–18 August. Chief engineer killed; UKMTO confirmed. Second vessel under the same management struck this month.
- **Seizure:** *AMARA* (IMO 9333280), Liberia-flagged chemical/products tanker, assessed seized after Iranian boarding near Qeshm Island on 17 August — in ballast, reportedly detained over transit authorisation and fee compliance.
- **Oil:** Brent US$93.62/bbl, WTI US$86.70/bbl and Murban US$101.00/bbl on 21 August, extending a four-day rally as the Hormuz impasse hardens.
- **Freight:** Baltic Dirty Tanker Index 2,894 (+70) with VLCC TCE up US$15,746 to US$266,512/day; TD2 (ME Gulf/Singapore) surged 53.22 Ws points to Ws528.44, equivalent to US$552,681/day.

## CEO Market Commentary

> **Capt. Dinesh Naidu KC — Founder & Chairman, DDS Marine Group**
>
> "A chief engineer went to work this week and did not come home. Before we talk about worldscale points, we should sit with that."

This publication exists to give operators a practical read on the market. This week the market delivered a crew fatality. A projectile entered the engine room of a Liberia-flagged bulk carrier half a nautical mile off the Omani coast, and the ship's chief engineer was killed. He was aboard a vessel that had been delivering food cargo to Iranian ports — not lifting energy cargo, not running a blockade, not obviously anyone's target. Every master and every superintendent reading this should take that fact seriously, because it means the targeting logic we have all been using to assess our own exposure no longer reliably holds.

The wider picture is that the 60-day MoU window lapsed on 17 August and Iran moved within two days to enforce a permit-and-toll regime, seizing the tanker *AMARA* near Qeshm Island for allegedly failing to meet transit authorisation and fee conditions. Washington rejects the regime's legitimacy. That leaves owners in an impossible position: comply with Tehran's demands and risk sanctions exposure, or refuse and risk seizure. There is no clean answer, and any master told to simply "use judgement" in that corridor has been failed by their shore organisation.

Watch what the largest fleets are doing rather than what anyone is saying. COSCO Shipping Energy Transportation and China Merchants Energy Shipping — together more than 100 VLCCs, and roughly half of China's pre-war Middle East crude imports — are reportedly keeping their ships out of both Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb on Beijing's guidance. Three China-linked tankers aborted transits inside the reporting window, one turning back mid-passage. When operators of that scale decline to transit despite VLCC TCE above US$266,000 a day, they are telling you something the freight indices cannot.

For Malaysia and Southeast Asia, our position is unchanged but the stakes have risen. Our waters are not the conflict zone; they are increasingly the alternative to it. That is commercially favourable and operationally demanding in equal measure. If we want the region to absorb redirected tonnage credibly, our STS execution, screening and documentation standards must be visibly better than the corridor everyone is avoiding. That is the opportunity, and it is also the obligation.

The rally is structural, not speculative. Brent has risen for four consecutive sessions and crossed US$90 on the day the MoU lapsed, closing the week above US$93 as Iran began enforcing its transit regime. That EIA reported a 4.4-million-barrel crude build for the week ending 14 August and prices rose anyway confirms what we said in Issue 003: this market is pricing chokepoint access, not inventory.

Murban through US$100 is the single most significant number on this page. A Gulf grade trading at a substantial premium to Brent — with ADNOC curbing supply to Asia and issuing its ninth spot tender since June — tells you the market is repricing the risk of physically getting a barrel out of the Gulf, not the barrel itself. Diesel remains the tightest part of the complex, with cracks around US$100 and commentary this week arguing the real crisis is in distillate rather than crude.

> **DDS BASE CASE**
>
> With the MoU expired and no negotiating framework in place, we see no near-term mechanism for de-escalation. Our base case is a sustained US$88–100/bbl Brent environment with sharp upside on any further seizure, fatality or terminal strike. The downside case now requires an entirely new diplomatic initiative, not the revival of a lapsed one.

**Commercial effect on shipping:** bunker costs are rising into an already elevated war-risk and insurance environment. Owners should assume voyage budgets built before 17 August are stale, and should re-price deviation, waiting time and security costs against the current regime.

## Crude Tanker Market

VLCC, Suezmax and Aframax directional assessment.

| Segment | Current Tone | Primary Driver |
|---|---|---|
| VLCC | Sharply firm / record territory | Scarcity of willing tonnage as Chinese fleets withdraw from Hormuz |
| Suezmax | Firm, Atlantic-led | TD20 WAF/UK-Cont +16.66; TD33 US Gulf/ARA +29.44 |
| Aframax | Mixed / softening | US Gulf legs easing; Black Sea strike risk persists |

**VLCC.** The Baltic Dirty Tanker Index rose 70 points to 2,894 on 18 August, driven almost entirely by the VLCC segment. TD2 (ME Gulf/Singapore) surged 53.22 Ws points to Ws528.44, worth US$552,681/day (+US$60,533) — the route most directly relevant to our readership. VLCC TCE across TD3C, TD15 and TD22 climbed US$15,746 to US$266,512/day, with reporting describing Gulf-to-China supertanker rates touching US$510,000/day and supertanker prices at record highs. Crucially, this is a scarcity rally driven by withdrawal of supply rather than growth in demand: with COSCO and China Merchants holding more than 100 VLCCs out of the corridor, the pool of owners willing to transit has shrunk faster than cargo has.

**Suezmax.** TCE rose to US$291,729/day (+US$1,681), now the highest-earning crude segment. The strength was Atlantic-led rather than Black Sea-led: TD20 (WAF/UK-Cont) jumped to Ws328.33 (+16.66) and TD33 (US Gulf/ARA) to Ws251.11 (+29.44), while TD6 (Black Sea/Med) actually eased to Ws553.89 (−6.67). That rotation is consistent with charterers pulling replacement barrels from the Atlantic Basin rather than accepting Black Sea loading risk — reinforced by the drone strike on *SKIROS* near Novorossiysk.

**Aframax.** The only crude segment to soften, with TCE down to US$82,647/day (−US$1,206). TD25 (USG/ARA) fell to Ws361.67, and TD9 (Caribs/USG) eased to Ws412.22, while TD14 (SE Asia/EC Australia) firmed modestly. The segment is caught between softening Americas short-haul demand and continued exposure to Black Sea and Red Sea security risk.

> **DDS VIEW**
>
> A US$15,746/day jump in VLCC TCE in one print is remarkable, but read the composition before celebrating. This is not cargo growth; it is the market repricing a shrinking pool of willing tonnage. Owners still prepared to transit Hormuz are earning extraordinary numbers precisely because so many are not. For charterers, the practical consequence is that nominal availability and executable availability have diverged further than at any point this year.

**The distillate squeeze.** The most important product-market development this week sits outside the freight indices. Commentary argued that the real crisis is not US$91 Brent but US$170 diesel, with diesel cracks around US$100 signalling a materially tighter market than crude prices alone suggest. EIA data showed distillate stocks sinking further even as crude inventories built. Russia's domestic fuel crisis has spread to Moscow with rationing reported, and Russia has taken its first gasoline cargo from India — an extraordinary reversal for a country that was a net product exporter.

> **DDS VIEW**
>
> Clean tanker rates softening while diesel cracks hit records is a contradiction worth understanding rather than dismissing. It tells us the constraint has moved upstream: there is no shortage of product tankers, there is a shortage of product to put in them and of safe routes to carry it on.

## Geopolitical & Route Risk

The Islamabad MoU expires — Hormuz, Red Sea, Black Sea and the Somali basin.

| Area | Current Signal | Commercial Consequence |

| Black Sea | *SKIROS* struck by Ukrainian drone near Novorossiysk 17 Aug; Russia retaliated against Odesa and Izmail. Global grain shipments down 8%. | Suezmax charterers rotating to Atlantic Basin replacement barrels. |
| Somali basin | Fifth vessel hijacked since 21 April (*LUTUF*, 17 Aug); four of five now held within ~130km along the Puntland coast. Over 40 seafarers held. | Renewed BMP hardening and armed-guard review required for Indian Ocean routings. |

**The 60-day ledger: what the MoU did and did not do.** Roughly 374 million barrels of crude cleared the Mideast Gulf across the 60-day window, about 6.1 mbd — nearly three times the blockaded pre-MoU pace of 2.3 mbd, but only around 40% of the ~15 mbd Hormuz averaged in 2025. The stranded backlog cleared: idle floating storage fell from 61 mb at signing to 16 mb within three weeks. The system did not heal. Total crude on water inside the Gulf closed the window at roughly 130 mb, above even the war's starting level of ~96 mb. Every operative commitment failed early: the US oil waiver survived 20 of the 60 days, the blockade lift 27, and Iran's mine-clearance obligation was never completed. Iran ends the window exporting less than it did during the war, with crude loadings collapsing from 893 kbd in July to 156 kbd through 17 August.

**The Strait went dark twice.** The transparency picture deteriorated as sharply as the volume picture. Cargo appearing in the Gulf of Oman with no confirmed upstream loading ran at around 28% before the MoU, fell to just 5% at the Week 3 peak as vessels loaded openly with AIS transmitting, then ratcheted back to 66% of a shrinking total in the expiry week. The Dark/Unknown share never fell below 44% and closed the window above 80%.

> **DDS RISK LEVEL: CRITICAL**
>
> Windward assesses the operational risk environment across Hormuz, the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, northern Arabian Gulf, Black Sea and Somali basin as critical. Iran is actively enforcing its transit and fee regime; kinetic incidents have produced crew fatalities; seizures have resumed after a two-month pause. DDS recommends no Hormuz transit without owner-level authorisation, current-week war-risk confirmation, and a documented position on Iran's permit-and-fee demands agreed with counsel and insurers in advance.

Obtain written owner and insurer positions before nominating tonnage.

Zombie tankers broadcast the IMO numbers of scrapped or dead vessels; one is using the IMO of a US-sanctioned Suezmax scrapped in November 2025.
