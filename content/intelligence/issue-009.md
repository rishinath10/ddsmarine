## This Week at a Glance

Last week the bypass went down. This week it came back — and was shot at. Saudi Arabia restarted the East-West pipeline on 22 September at a low rate, and two days later Houthi forces fired six ballistic missiles at Saudi targets including Yanbu, the Red Sea terminal the pipeline feeds. All were reported intercepted, but by Thursday no tanker had yet loaded at Yanbu. Meanwhile the Kingdom kept moving crude through Hormuz via ship-to-ship transfers off Oman, which pulled roughly 40 VLCCs into a job that needed 24 in August and pushed VLCC earnings to a reported record near US$1.27 million a day. Oil briefly fell below US$100 on talk of a phased US–Iran deal to reopen Hormuz, then recovered to a weekly gain for Brent. For our region the practical message is unchanged: risk has not left the Gulf, it has spread along both of Saudi Arabia's coasts.

- **Bypass back, under fire:** the East-West pipeline restarted on 22 Sep at a low rate; on 24 Sep six Houthi missiles aimed at Yanbu, Taif and other targets were reported intercepted; no Yanbu loadings confirmed yet.
- **Record VLCC earnings:** about US$1.27m/day on 21 Sep (LSEG via Reuters) as Saudi STS volumes off Oman rose to about 3.6m bpd.
- **Hormuz traffic still in single digits:** 9 commodity vessels crossed on Thursday (Reuters), against a 10-day average of 17–18 and a pre-conflict norm above 125.

## CEO Market Commentary

> **Capt. Dinesh Naidu KC — Founder & Chairman, DDS Marine Group**
>
> "The bypass is back. The risk simply moved to the other coast."

Last week I wrote that the pipeline built to let Saudi Arabia avoid Hormuz had become a casualty of the war it was built to avoid. This week the pipeline came back: Aramco restarted it on Tuesday, at a low rate, and a first cargo was scheduled to load at Yanbu for China. By Thursday flows were building, but Kpler data show the two tankers due to load on 23 September did not. Meanwhile, Houthi missiles were fired at Yanbu. They were intercepted. But the market now has to price a Red Sea export route that is running again and is also a target.

What interests me more than the headlines is what Saudi Arabia did in the meantime. With Yanbu shut, the Kingdom sent crude back through Hormuz and transferred it ship-to-ship off Sohar in Oman — more than 60 million barrels sold for this kind of transfer, according to Reuters. Kpler puts September STS volumes at about 3.6 million barrels a day, up from 0.9 million in August. That is why VLCC rates set another record this week. The ships are not carrying more oil around the world; they are queuing longer to move it a short distance. STS operations near Hormuz are now taking close to ten days, where five to seven was normal.

For DDS, that point matters. Ship-to-ship transfer is our trade. What we are seeing off Oman is a live demonstration that when routes break, STS capacity — safe, well-planned, properly documented STS capacity — becomes the bottleneck of the whole system. The operators who can run it cleanly will be the ones charterers call first. In our own corridor that means planning, compatibility checks and documentation discipline, not only speed.

On prices, I would caution against reading too much into one week's moves. Brent fell to US$97.69 on Tuesday on talk of a phased US–Iran deal, then recovered to about US$105 by Friday. Nothing on the water has changed yet: Hormuz traffic is still in single digits most days, and the list of vessels Iran says it will act against has not shrunk. Until ships actually move, we plan on the basis of the risk we can see, not the deal we are told about.

## This Week in Review

The week's principal developments, 19–25 September 2026.

**22 September · Reuters / Hydrocarbon Processing — Saudi Arabia restarts the East-West pipeline.** Saudi Arabia restarted the East-West pipeline on Tuesday, nine days after drone attacks on pumping stations forced it to shut on 13 September. It was pumping at a low rate after the restart; Aramco is reported to be aiming for its normal ~4m bpd, and a security source said full resumption could take weeks. By Thursday Reuters reported flows building towards Yanbu, but Kpler data showed the two tankers due to load there on 23 September did not (via Integr8). About 20 tankers were reported waiting in the Red Sea.

**24 September · Al Jazeera / Bloomberg — Houthi missiles fired at Yanbu and Taif; all reported intercepted.** The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted six ballistic missiles launched by the Houthis at targets including Taif and Yanbu. The Houthis said they targeted Aramco facilities in Yanbu and a "sensitive target" in Riyadh. Civil-defence alerts were issued for Mecca, Jeddah, Tabuk, Yanbu and Taif, then lifted. The reports we reviewed did not describe damage to export facilities.

**21–25 September · Reuters / LSEG / Kpler (via oilprice.com, MarineLink) — VLCC earnings set a record as Oman STS capacity maxes out.** VLCC earnings reached about US$1.27m/day on Monday, per LSEG data, easing only slightly to US$1.26m later in the week. Saudi crude moved by STS off Oman averaged about 3.6m bpd in September against 0.9m bpd in August (Kpler), needing about 40 VLCCs instead of 24 (Oil Brokerage). Vortexa said STS queues near Hormuz are lengthening, with operations taking close to ten days.

**22–25 September · oilprice.com / Iran International / Bloomberg — phased US–Iran deal to reopen Hormuz under discussion.** A senior Iranian official told Kyodo that Hormuz could reopen within seven days if the US ended its blockade of Iranian ports and halted military operations. Brent fell below US$100 on the report. Qatar-mediated talks followed on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, with both sides reported to be exploring a phased arrangement. No agreement had been announced at our cut-off.

**23 September · Al Arabiya / UKMTO — bulk carrier struck in the Strait of Hormuz.** The bulk carrier Cape Dao, bound for India, was hit by an unknown projectile off Musandam. Oman's Maritime Security Centre evacuated the 27 crew; the Indian embassy confirmed one Indian seafarer lost his life. Our thoughts are with his family and colleagues.

This was a week of two-way risk. On Tuesday a senior Iranian official floated reopening Hormuz within seven days if the US lifted its blockade of Iranian ports; combined with the East-West pipeline restart the same day, that took Brent below US$100 for the first time since 9 September. By Thursday, Houthi missiles fired at Yanbu, and reports that the talks might be phased rather than immediate, had pushed it back up to about US$107 before it settled near US$105 on Friday.

Underneath the headlines, supply is still tight. Saudi output was reported at its lowest since 1990, US strategic reserves remain near record lows, and diesel is the tightest part of the barrel: US retail diesel is at about US$6.50 a gallon, Russia has banned diesel exports through September, and India says it will keep exporting despite the shortage. The Federal Reserve's rate rise last week adds a demand-side headwind, but not yet enough to offset the supply picture.

## Geopolitical & Route Risk

Hormuz and the Red Sea — both Saudi export routes now carry active military risk.

**Two coasts, one risk.** For most of this year the Red Sea route out of Yanbu was the safe alternative to Hormuz. This week showed that it is not safe, only different. The Houthis, who declared a naval blockade in July and have since taken control of Yemen's western coast (Al Jazeera), have moved from ships to the terminal itself. At the same time, the Hormuz route that Saudi Arabia fell back on is congested and still sees attacks, including the strike on Cape Dao on 23 September.

The phased-deal talks are the main upside risk. Iran has signalled that Hormuz could reopen within a week if the US ends its blockade of Iranian ports; a White House official said the US had "little need to negotiate" given its position. Until traffic numbers actually rise, we treat the talks as sentiment, not supply.

**Hormuz daily commodity-vessel transits:** 9 on Thursday and 11 on Wednesday (Reuters), against a 10-day average of 17–18 and a pre-conflict norm above 125 (The National, UANI).

## Sanctions & Compliance Watch

**Developments — week ending 25 September 2026**

Iran's blacklist holds at 77–78 vessels. Windward counts 77 vessels on the Persian Gulf Strait Authority list, about 85% of them tankers and roughly 40% Emirati-owned or chartered. Issue 008 reported 78; the difference most likely reflects how duplicate or removed listings are counted. Windward's assessment is that listed ships are not being deterred, which increases the risk of enforcement action by Iran against individual vessels.

US blockade of Iranian ports continues. UANI reports that US forces had redirected 110 commercial vessels, disabled three and boarded two as of 21 September, and that at least 64 tankers loaded with Iranian oil are clustered along Iran's coast.

OFAC: Treasury issued Iran-related designations on 17 September, after our last cut-off. No new Iran-related vessel actions appeared on OFAC's recent-actions list between 18 and 25 September.

Cyber: reporting from Cybernews on 21 September adds detail to the tanker cyber incidents covered in Issue 008. The FBI and US Coast Guard are examining reports that attackers briefly affected safety and control systems on an LNG carrier and two Texas-bound tankers, and are investigating possible links to Iran.

Direct operational implication for DDS: UANI reports 14 Iranian-flagged cargo ships anchored or loitering off Malaysia's eastern OPL, and two named vessels at Port Klang. Screening of counterparties and vessels in our own waters is no longer a formality.
