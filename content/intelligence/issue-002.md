## This Week at a Glance

Oil has fallen sharply since our last issue — Brent is off more than eleven percent from its 31 July close as markets price in reports of a draft de-escalation arrangement around the Strait of Hormuz. But the physical market has not caught up with the financial one. Hormuz tanker traffic remains stalled near a two-month low despite the reported peace signals, Ukraine's maritime drone campaign has cut dirty tanker loadings from Russian Black Sea and Sea of Azov ports by 62% in the last two weeks of July, and UN trade data shows Hormuz-dependent economies still running at roughly half their normal export volumes. For Southeast Asian operators, falling headline crude prices should not be read as a falling risk premium. Screening, war-risk allocation and route documentation remain conditions precedent on any Gulf, Red Sea or Black Sea-linked fixture.

- **Oil:** Brent US$79.18/bbl and WTI US$74.80/bbl on 6 August, extending a sharp slide from US$89.68/US$86.80 at the 31 July close as reports emerge of a draft Hormuz agreement awaiting approval from Iran's Supreme Leader.
- **Bunkers:** Singapore VLSFO US$844.50/mt and MGO US$1,205.00/mt, still trading well inside their recent US$797–873/mt and US$1,127–1,355/mt ranges despite the crude pullback.
- **Freight:** The Baltic Dry Index rose for a fifth straight session to 3,063 points, a two-month high, led by a 6.6% surge in the Capesize segment.
- **Compliance:** OFAC (29 July) designated two Iran-linked "insurance" entities and specific tankers behind a mandatory Hormuz transit scheme.

## CEO Market Commentary

> **Capt. Dinesh Naidu KC — Founder & Chairman, DDS Marine Group**
>
> "A falling oil price is not the same thing as a falling risk. This week, the gap between the two is the whole story."

Since our last issue, the tanker market has been handed a genuinely rare event: a double-digit percentage fall in crude prices inside a single week, driven not by a demand shock but by reported diplomatic progress on the Strait of Hormuz. That is unambiguously good news if it holds. It is not, on its own, a reason to relax operational discipline.

Three things should keep owners, charterers and service providers in Southeast Asia cautious. First, Hormuz tanker traffic itself has not moved with the price — reporting this week describes transits stalling near a two-month low even as peace-talk headlines circulate, which tells us the people actually moving ships through the Strait are still pricing real risk, not headline risk. Second, Ukraine's escalating maritime drone campaign in the Black Sea and Sea of Azov has cut Russian and Kazakh dirty tanker loadings by well over half in a fortnight, is reportedly reaching further offshore toward Western-flagged tonnage, and is already showing up in wider Cape of Good Hope routing. Third, UN trade-agency data this week put hard numbers on what six months of Hormuz disruption has actually done to physical trade — a 54% year-on-year fall in combined export volumes of twelve strategic energy, fertilizer and industrial products from the Gulf's Hormuz-dependent economies.

For Malaysia and the wider Southeast Asian region, the practical implication is unchanged from Issue 001: this remains a risk-allocation market before it is a freight market.

The scale of this week's move is unusual. Brent has fallen from a 31 July close of US$89.68/bbl to US$79.18/bbl on 6 August — a decline of close to twelve percent in under a week — on reporting of a draft Hormuz de-escalation arrangement reportedly awaiting approval from Iran's Supreme Leader, alongside an earlier halt to threatened Iran strikes. Markets have moved quickly to price out geopolitical risk premium.

The physical market has been slower to follow. Hormuz tanker traffic is described as remaining subdued and stalling near a two-month low even as peace-talk headlines circulate — a signal that commercial operators, insurers and flag administrations are still treating the Strait as high-risk pending confirmed, durable de-escalation.

**Commercial effect on shipping:** a fast, large downward move in crude and bunker costs improves voyage economics and working-capital requirements in the near term, but should not be read as a signal to loosen war-risk, insurance or routing terms in charter-parties.

VLCC, Suezmax and Aframax directional assessment.

| Segment | Current Tone | Primary Driver |

| Suezmax | Volatile, route-disrupted | Black Sea/Sea of Azov drone campaign; Cape diversions widening |
| Aframax | Firm, regionally exposed | Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb Houthi activity; Black Sea dislocation |

**VLCC.** Hormuz tanker traffic is stalling at a two-month low even as reports circulate of a draft de-escalation arrangement reportedly awaiting approval from Iran's Supreme Leader. That divergence — falling headline oil prices against traffic that has not recovered — is the defining feature of the VLCC market this week. Owners with clean compliance histories and documented routing continue to command a premium.

**Suezmax.** The segment is being reshaped by the Black Sea. BIMCO reports that dirty tanker loadings from Russian Black Sea and Sea of Azov ports fell 62% in the final two weeks of July compared with the prior four-week average — from 2.59 million barrels per day down to 0.98 million bpd — following Ukraine's Operation MoLoChKa maritime drone campaign, which began in the Sea of Azov on 6 July and expanded into the Black Sea on 15 July. Kazakh dirty exports via the CPC Novorossiysk terminal fell by a similar 62%.

**Aframax.** Firm and regionally exposed, tracking both the Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb Houthi activity and the Black Sea dislocation described above.

## Clean & Product Tankers

Refining-margin support for the product tanker complex remains intact. The ongoing Middle East conflict has tightened global fuel supplies and pushed refining margins to record highs, with Strait of Hormuz disruption, reduced Asian refining runs and export restrictions squeezing diesel, gasoline and jet-fuel markets simultaneously. Separately, extreme heat and drought across Europe are cutting refinery efficiency and forcing cuts to nuclear and hydropower generation — a second, independent driver pushing diesel refining margins toward multi-decade highs.

| Segment | Opportunity | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| LR2 | Long-haul middle-distillate and naphtha movements benefiting from Middle East/Asia dislocation and record refining margins. | High bunker exposure; route security overlap with Aframax trading economics. |
| MR | Flexible regional distribution and replacement cargoes as refiners reroute around Hormuz-linked supply gaps. | Port congestion and fast-changing arbitrage economics as crude benchmarks swing sharply week to week. |
| Handy / Specialised | Regional supply, chemicals and smaller-port access, less exposed to headline Gulf volatility. | Cargo compatibility, vetting and tank-cleanliness documentation remain the binding constraints. |

> **DDS VIEW**
>
> The product tanker story this week is really two independent supply shocks arriving together — Middle East conflict-driven refining disruption, and a European heatwave cutting refinery efficiency and power availability at the same time. Both push in the same direction: tighter refined-product supply and firmer long-haul tonne-mile demand for LR2 and MR tonnage.

## Bunker Market — Singapore & Global Benchmark

Singapore VLSFO stood at US$844.50/mt on 6 August, up US$4.50 day-on-day and comfortably within its recent US$797–873/mt trading range even as crude benchmarks fell sharply — a reminder that bunker pricing lags and only partially tracks crude. MGO stood at US$1,205.00/mt (recent range US$1,127–1,355/mt) and IFO380 at US$588.00/mt (recent range US$572.50–600.00/mt).

Asia/Pacific bunker prices are broadly firmer week-on-week even as the Americas region has pulled back, consistent with continued tightness in Asian product supply. Singapore's VLSFO sits mid-pack among Asia/Pacific hubs this week — a premium to Fujairah (US$804.50) and a discount to Hong Kong (US$850.00) — while its premium to European and US Gulf ports remains wide.

A compliance-cost note: Ship & Bunker's EU ETS series puts the EUA-VLSFO equivalent surcharge at roughly US$301/mt this week (EUA at €81.60) — a material addition to voyage cost for EU-linked calls that should be reflected explicitly in bunker and freight clauses.

## Sanctions & Compliance Watch

**Regulatory developments — week ending 6 August 2026**

On 29 July 2026, US Treasury (OFAC) designated Persian Gulf Marine Insurance Company and HormuzSafe Marine Services Authority — alleged IRGC-linked entities operating a mandatory maritime "insurance" scheme that forces vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz to purchase IRGC-brokered coverage, including digital-asset payment channels designed to evade sanctions. The same action designated tankers including YEHOPE (IMO 9243320, Barbados-flagged) and WELL SAIL (IMO 9321938, Marshall Islands-flagged), plus further vessels linked to the Mohammad Hossein Shamkhani network, which Treasury says has now seen over 200 individuals, entities and vessels sanctioned in total. OFAC's action log through early August shows sustained high-tempo activity — a reminder that Gulf-linked sanctions exposure moves quickly and needs re-screening close to each transaction.

## Southeast Asia & STS Operations

This week's global data reinforces, rather than changes, the structural case set out in Issue 001: as Middle East and Black Sea routing both become less predictable, Southeast Asia's position astride the Strait of Malacca and the Singapore Strait gives it an early, practical read on how owners and charterers are actually adjusting. This week's evidence includes Thailand's 62% increase in Hormuz-linked imports as refiners diversified sourcing, the widening use of Cape of Good Hope routing for cargo that would once have transited the Red Sea or Black Sea directly, and Singapore's continued position as a comparatively firm Asia/Pacific bunkering hub even as prices in the Americas have pulled back.

## Freight & Rate Signals

The Baltic Exchange's dry bulk freight index rose for a fifth consecutive session, climbing 4.3% to 3,063 points — its highest level since 3 June — led by the larger vessel segments. The Capesize index surged 6.6% to 5,094 points, its highest since 3 June, while the Panamax index rose 2.2% to 2,236 points, its highest since 17 July. While the BDI tracks dry bulk rather than tanker tonnage directly, the broad-based strength in the larger segments is a useful cross-check on underlying global trade volumes at a moment when tanker markets are being driven primarily by geopolitical, rather than demand-side, factors.

As detailed in the Crude Tanker Market section above, BIMCO estimates that if the reduced loading rate from Russian Black Sea and Sea of Azov ports persists, global dirty tanker volumes could fall a further 3%, on top of a 5.6% year-on-year decline already recorded in 2026 — a supply-side tightening story running in parallel with, and largely independent of, this week's sharp fall in headline crude prices.
