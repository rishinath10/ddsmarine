## This Week at a Glance

The tanker complex remains dominated by geopolitical risk rather than a conventional seasonal pattern. Crude prices have eased from the week's spike, but the operational risk premium remains embedded in voyage planning, insurance, bunkers and vessel availability. Red Sea and Hormuz uncertainty continues to support longer-routing scenarios and can tighten effective tonnage supply even when headline cargo flows remain intact. For Southeast Asian operators, the immediate commercial focus is disciplined counterparty screening, documented AIS and routing decisions, verified insurance, sanctions checks immediately before fixture and operation, and explicit allocation of war-risk costs in charter-party and service contracts.

- **Oil:** Brent US$85.98/bbl and WTI US$82.47/bbl on 31 July, after an exceptionally volatile month.
- **Bunkers:** Singapore VLSFO US$830.50/mt and MGO US$1,224.50/mt on 30 July; fuel costs remain a major voyage-economics variable.
- **Fleet positioning:** 728 tankers tracked around the Arabian Gulf in Week 29, including 123 VLCCs, 34 Suezmaxes and 18 Aframax/LR2 units in the crude segment.
- **Compliance:** OFAC added 10 entities and 8 tankers in an Iran-related action on 29 July; screening must be repeated close to execution.
- **Route security:** Tankers carrying Saudi crude to India switched off AIS while transiting the Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb for stated safety reasons.

Freight, insurance, bunkers, routing, sanctions exposure and operational readiness are now moving together. A fixture that appears attractive on headline freight can become commercially weak if war-risk premiums, deviation time, security requirements or delayed port clearances are not allocated clearly before performance.

The Middle East remains the centre of global crude tanker risk. However, the impact is international: Asian refiners are adjusting procurement terms, owners are reassessing routes, insurers are repricing exposure, and charterers are demanding greater documentary certainty. The result is a market in which dependable operational execution is as valuable as access to tonnage.

For Malaysia and the wider Southeast Asian region, this creates both responsibility and opportunity. The region can strengthen its role as a trusted maritime and energy-services hub by combining safe STS execution, qualified local marine personnel, robust vessel screening, transparent documentation and practical coordination between owners, charterers, ports, surveyors, pilots and service providers.

The decline in crude prices at month-end does not signal the removal of geopolitical risk. It reflects the market's judgment that oil continues to move despite conflict, alternative routes and defensive maritime measures. The physical market remains tight enough that refinery outages and security incidents can produce sharp product-price reactions even when crude benchmarks retreat.

The latest US inventory draw was materially larger than market expectations. Combined with refinery utilisation above 97%, this points to strong system throughput and limited tolerance for further supply or logistics disruption.

> **DDS BASE CASE**
>
> Oil prices remain highly volatile with a persistent geopolitical premium. A temporary easing in hostilities could pressure crude lower, but any confirmed disruption to Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb, major export terminals or refining capacity would likely reverse that movement quickly.

**Commercial effect on shipping:** Higher crude and bunker prices increase voyage working capital and can widen the financial consequences of delays. Owners and charterers should verify bunker clauses, deviation treatment, war-risk premiums and additional cost allocation before fixing.

## Crude Tanker Market

VLCC, Suezmax and Aframax directional assessment.

| Segment | Current Tone | Primary Driver |
|---|---|---|
| VLCC | Firm / highly volatile | AG security, voyage length, tonnage positioning |
| Suezmax | Firm | Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb and Atlantic alternatives |
| Aframax | Firm but regional | Mediterranean, Red Sea, Black Sea and regional crude flows |

**VLCC.** Public fleet-tracking analysis for Week 29 identified 123 VLCCs within the crude tanker population around the Arabian Gulf, representing 68.7% of crude tankers recorded in that area. This concentration means that any restriction, security delay or insurance constraint around Hormuz can rapidly affect a large share of global crude-carrying capacity. The commercial signal is supportive but unstable: owners with acceptable risk profiles and clear routing can command a premium, while charterers must distinguish between nominal vessel availability and vessels that are operationally, legally and commercially executable.

**Suezmax.** Demand is being supported by the segment's ability to move approximately one-million-barrel parcels and by route changes affecting Red Sea, Mediterranean and Atlantic Basin flows. A reported Suezmax voyage carrying Saudi crude to India — including a period without public AIS transmission for stated safety reasons — highlights the operational complexity now affecting this segment.

**Aframax.** Earnings remain strongly linked to regional dislocation. The segment can benefit from replacement barrels, port limitations and shorter-haul movements, but it is also highly exposed to local security and congestion. Prompt position and a clean compliance history are increasingly important differentiators.

> **DDS VIEW**
>
> Our assessment is that VLCC freight is likely to remain supported over the coming two weeks. Effective tonnage supply is being constrained less by fleet size than by which vessels can actually be executed safely and compliantly through the Gulf and Red Sea corridors.

## Clean & Product Tankers

LR2, MR and refining-margin implications. Product tanker fundamentals are receiving support from refinery disruption and exceptionally strong fuel cracks — European diesel refining margins are near record levels, with elevated gasoline and jet-fuel margins as refinery outages reduce available product supply.

| Segment | Opportunity | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| LR2 | Long-haul middle-distillate and naphtha movements; benefits from Middle East/Asia dislocation. | High bunker exposure; route security; overlap with Aframax trading economics. |
| MR | Flexible regional distribution and replacement cargoes. | Port congestion, volatile prompt lists and fast-changing arbitrage economics. |
| Handy / Specialised | Regional supply, chemicals and smaller-port access. | Cargo compatibility, vetting, tank cleanliness and parcel-specific documentation. |

> **DDS VIEW**
>
> The key product-tanker question is whether high refining margins translate into sustained export runs or are offset by refinery outages and feedstock constraints. In the near term, supply dislocation is supportive for tonne-mile demand, particularly where replacement products must move from more distant refining centres.

**Operating checklist**

- Confirm cargo specification and compatibility before nomination.
- Validate terminal restrictions, manifold size and reducer requirements.
- Recheck sanctions and beneficial ownership immediately before fixture and loading.
- Allocate additional war-risk and deviation costs explicitly in the charter-party.

## Geopolitical & Route Risk

Hormuz, Red Sea, Bab el-Mandeb and Black Sea.

| Area | Current Signal | Commercial Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Strait of Hormuz | Traffic remains possible but security and sanctions risk remain elevated. | Insurance, routing approval, crew welfare, owner consent and possible delay. |
| Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb | Saudi cargoes and other vessels face Houthi-related threat and rerouting decisions. | Longer voyages, AIS/security dilemmas, possible delivered-basis procurement. |
| Black Sea / CPC | Attacks and terminal interruptions threaten export reliability. | Replacement barrels, voyage disruption and higher regional tanker demand. |
| Eastern Mediterranean | Security spillover and refinery disruption affect products and insurance. | Higher product cracks, altered flows and additional voyage due diligence. |

**AIS and war-zone operations.** AIS transmission decisions in conflict zones create a difficult balance. Safety considerations may justify temporary measures under applicable rules and flag/company procedures, but unexplained AIS gaps are also a recognised sanctions and compliance red flag. Every decision should be documented by the master and company, supported by a formal risk assessment.

**Charter-party protection.** Contracts should identify who bears additional war-risk premium, Kidnap & Ransom cover, security personnel, deviation fuel, time lost awaiting routing permission, convoy or escort requirements, and any additional port or agency costs. General wording is often insufficient during fast-moving security events.

> **DDS RISK LEVEL: HIGH**
>
> The market remains executable, but no Middle East or Red Sea fixture should proceed on commercial assumptions alone. Operational, insurance, and sanctions checks are conditions precedent, not formalities.
