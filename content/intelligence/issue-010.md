## This Week at a Glance

The Gulf is shipping more oil than at any point since the war began, and the risk of shipping it has not fallen. Middle East exports reached about 12.8 million barrels a day in September, Yanbu loadings resumed, and Indian refiners have started chartering ships to lift Gulf crude through Hormuz themselves. In the same week three tankers were struck in the Strait, the US ordered a third aircraft carrier towards Iran, and China halted most October fuel exports. G7 governments responded to the diesel shortage with a 100-million-barrel reserve release, which briefly pushed Brent below US$100. For our corridor the message is: volumes are coming back faster than safety is, and the diesel shortage is now a global problem, not only a Gulf one.

## This Week in Review

The week's principal developments, 26 September – 2 October 2026.

**28–30 September · Reuters / Kpler (via oilprice.com) — Middle East oil exports reach their highest since the war began.** Gulf oil exports averaged about 12.8m bpd in September, against roughly 17m bpd before the war, with Saudi Arabia at about 5.4m bpd. Kpler put Hormuz flows at about 77% of pre-war levels. LNG lagged: about 21 cargoes passed through Hormuz in September, against more than 90 a month before the war, and Qatar has extended force majeure through November.

**29–30 September · Seatrade / MarineLink / TankerTrackers — Yanbu loadings resume after the pipeline restart.** Loadings at Yanbu restarted late in the week of 22 September. Satellite imagery showed nearly 10m barrels being loaded at Yanbu and Al Muajjiz on 27 September. Pipeline throughput is reported at 2–2.65m bpd, against a 7m bpd capacity, and full recovery could take another month. A VLCC fixture from the Red Sea to South Korea was reported this week (see Crude Tanker Market).

**29–30 September · UKMTO / Marisks / Reuters / Windward — three tankers struck in the Strait of Hormuz.** Al Ruwais and Mersin Prosperity, both managed by ADNOC Logistics & Services, and the Aframax Sinbad, managed by Anglo-Eastern, were hit by unknown projectiles. All three are Liberia-flagged. One fire was extinguished and all crew are safe. Windward reports that at least two were transiting with AIS switched off.

**1–2 October · oilprice.com / Bloomberg / NBC / The National — diesel crisis: China halts exports, G7 releases reserves.** China's major refiners suspended most October fuel exports to protect domestic supply; Kpler estimates China's diesel stocks are about 20m barrels below pre-war levels. Russia extended its producers' diesel and marine fuel export ban to 31 October. On 2 October the G7 agreed to release up to 100m barrels over four months — 50m of diesel from Europe and 50m of crude through the IEA.

**1–2 October · Al Jazeera / Bloomberg / Axios / US Treasury — US builds up forces and widens sanctions.** A third carrier group and 9,000–10,000 more sailors and Marines have been ordered towards Iran, and two more Patriot batteries were sent to protect Saudi and Qatari energy sites. Washington rejected Iran's seven-day roadmap to reopen Hormuz and sanctioned Iran's automotive, rail and metals sectors on 1 October. Indirect talks continue without a breakthrough.

Crude and diesel are now telling different stories. Crude supply is improving: Gulf exports are at their highest since the war began, and Kpler puts Hormuz flows at about 77% of pre-war levels. That, plus the G7 release, is why Brent ended the week lower despite the US military build-up and fresh tanker strikes.

Diesel is the tightest part of the barrel. China has halted most October fuel exports, Russia's ban runs to 31 October, and several Middle East and Russian refineries remain damaged. Before the G7 announcement, European diesel futures were above US$200 a barrel and the diesel premium over crude was about US$77; it narrowed to about US$69 afterwards (oilprice.com). The release adds barrels but, as oilprice.com notes, "does not add refinery capacity." OPEC+ meets on 4 October and is expected to leave quotas unchanged.

> **DDS BASE CASE**
>
> Brent trades in a US$95–108 band. Rising Gulf exports and the G7 release cap the upside; the US build-up and continuing tanker strikes support the floor.

**Commercial effect on shipping:** diesel, not crude, is now driving product-tanker earnings and bunker costs. Budget gasoil-linked costs on a range, and expect further volatility.

## Sanctions & Compliance Watch

**Developments — week ending 2 October 2026**

Iran's blacklist rises to 81 vessels. The Persian Gulf Strait Authority added four ships, including two Sinokor VLCCs and a Bahri VLCC, and warned that charterers who press owners to transit without its approval will have "all their vessels' transits restricted" (Seatrade, 28 September). OFAC has separately warned that complying with Iran's procedures may itself breach US sanctions.

US Treasury, 1 October: new sanctions target Iran's automotive, rail, metals and steel sectors. No vessels were designated in this action.

US blockade: UANI reports 125 commercial vessels redirected since 14 July, three disabled and two boarded. No crude-laden Iranian tanker has been tracked past the blockade since 12 July.

Charterer exposure: the PGSA warning is new. Charterers, not only owners, can now be listed. Combined with OFAC's position, this leaves charterers facing conflicting demands from Tehran and Washington on the same voyage.

Direct operational implication for DDS: UANI reports that Malaysian authorities carried out enforcement action at the eastern OPL anchorage in September, after which about 26 sanctions-linked tankers moved north. Around 18–19 such tankers remain at the EOPL, together with Iran-flagged ships. Screening in our own waters remains essential.
